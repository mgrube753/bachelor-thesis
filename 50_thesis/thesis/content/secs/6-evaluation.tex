\subsection{First Experiment}
\label{sec:first-experiment}

The first experiment of the thesis focused on evaluating the content fidelity of questions generated by different \ac{llms} using two distinct prompt strategies: a common prompt and a complex prompt. In this experiment, the goal was to determine whether the complexity of the prompt influences the content fidelity and final scoring of the generated questions, addressed in the first research question:

\requ{1}{To what extent do \ac{llms} adhere to the content of diverse provided instructional texts when generating questions?} Experiments 1a and 1b follow both quantitative and qualitative approaches each to evaluate the generated questions properly. The quantitative analysis solely focuses on the analysis of content fidelity, while the qualitative analysis assesses the quality of the generated questions based on a certain rubric. This rubric also covers the question's \textit{Correctness}, which provides an underlying criterion for assessing content fidelity on expert-level.

\paragraph*{Quantitative Analysis} 

Two metrics were employed to assess content adherence: the calculation of \textit{Semantic Similarity} using cosine similarity between resulting semantic vectors -- one for the source material file, and one for the question file -- from the embedding model, and \ac{llm}-based \textit{Adherence Score} analysis between these files as an alternate approach to evaluate content fidelity.

\object{Experiment 1a -- Content Fidelity} The manner of evaluation revealed several key patterns in \ac{llm} behavior. The question type for each question was not explicitly constrained, but to provide a valid analysis by the experts, the questions' output formats were limited to either Open-Ended or Multiple-Choice questions. This resulted in a trend toward \ac{mcqs}, with only 9 out of 168 questions being Open-Ended in Experiment 1a -- with 8 questions from OpenAI, and 1 from Google --, based on the complex prompt. With regard to this finding, the trend was consistent across the \ac{llm} set, demonstrating an inherent bias toward structured question formats.

As an introductory step, to observe if both approaches are valid, the \ac{llms} were prompted to generate a question for each ISO-OSI layer without using an input source. Here, a key observation is that the cosine similarity approach did not reliably distinguish between questions generated with or without input sources. As shown in Figure \bfref{fig:exp1a-source-scores}, the scores for the ones without a source were similar to those using actual source material, indicating that cosine similarity is not a robust indicator of content fidelity for these kinds of texts. In contrast, the adherence score provided a clearer differentiation. Using no source led to noticeably lower adherence overall in the median, the mean and a broader range of scores, confirming that this metric better presents whether the model follows the source material or not. Additionally, a certain trend in cosine similarity across the sources is also visible in Figure \bfref{fig:exp1a-llm-source-comparison}. There is to be seen that the precise script is indicating best results, Tanenbaum excerpts are in the middle, and the long transcript parts equipped with examples are the ones with the widest range of scores, the lowest median and mean values. This trend appears to be influenced by the amount of words in the source materials rather than content quality, limiting its overall usefulness. The adherence scores showed some outliers, but the scoring in this heatmap shows individual \ac{llm} performance according to the diverse input sources, overall above 90\%.

Regarding the observations solely sorted by the \ac{llms}, the results in Figure \bfref{fig:exp1a-llm-scores} also reveal that the cosine similarity scores were generally lower and also differ a lot from the adherence scores. Supported by the expert results by the primary thesis supervisor, the cosine similarity scores were not reliable for this task, as they did not properly reflect the content fidelity of the generated questions in Experiment 1a. The adherence scores, however, provided a more nuanced view of model performance, showing that DeepSeek and OpenAI were more consistent in adhering to the content, while Anthropic and Google exhibited slightly lower and more variable scores.

These findings suggest that, while cosine similarity may be suitable for short, sentence-level comparisons -- as the \textit{Sentence Transformer} expression suggests --, it is less appropriate for evaluating content fidelity between generated questions and also long, more complex educational texts. Consistent context e.g.\ up to 30 words might improve cosine similarity's reliability, but in this setting, it will be disregarded as a primary metric for Experiment 1a, since the thesis deals with texts which are longer than a single sentence and contain complex information. As an example, \cite{li_planning_2024} used BERTScore for semantic similarity scoring, but between generated and ground truth question-answer pairs. This is not the case in this thesis, since the source texts and \ac{llm} outputs differ significantly.

Some examples of such questions are \enquote{Which of the following statements describes a real core task of the Data Link Layer in the ISO-OSI model, based on correct technical knowledge?} or \enquote{Which property is NOT assigned to the Transport Layer according to the description?}.

Overall, the high cosine similarity scores in Experiment 1b are not reliable for this kind of evaluation. The adherence scores instead illustrate a reliable course in the plots, showing that Google and Anthropic sticked more often to the manipulated content, while OpenAI and especially DeepSeek often recognized the manipulation and generated questions where this aspect is understandable for both humans and \ac{llms}. In this subexperiment, DeepSeek -- as the oldest of the four chosen models -- also convinced with the lowest median, mean adherence scores and a wide range of values, indicating that it often recognized the manipulation.

Furthermore, the used type of prompt had an impact on the results, as shown in Figure \bfref{fig:exp1b-llm-prompt-comparison}. In this case, both cosine similarity and adherence scores indicate that the common prompt led to higher results than the complex prompt. As stated, the cosine similarity cannot reliably determine if an \ac{llm} follows the manipulated content or if it detects it and generates a question based on that text. In the end, the adherence scores show more reliable results, indicating that the complex prompt led to lower scores, as the models often questioned the manipulated content. Like in Experiment 1a, DeepSeek and OpenAI were more convincing in their performance, while Anthropic and Google showed more high adherence scores.
